## 1. World & Tone

### Setting
A bronze age fantasy world — ancient in feel, drawing from the spirit of early Mesopotamia, the Aegean Bronze Age, and the ancient Near East, filtered through a fantasy lens. Not a faithful historical simulation, but everything should *feel* old. Iron is rare and sacred. Cities are walled. The gods are real, and the line between ritual and magic is thin.

### The City-State — [City Name TBD]
A prosperous, stratified society built on a river in the desert. The city sits against a large river — not the ocean, but large enough for significant trade. The surrounding land is marginally fertile by the river's grace; beyond that, desert.

The city's outer wall is ancient beyond memory. It is larger in scale than anything current inhabitants could build. Nobody knows who made it. The palace is constructed from refurbished ruins and is physically part of the old structure — its foundations are something else's foundations. The whole city is squatting in the bones of something it does not understand.

### Tone
Grounded, gritty, and mythic. Magic is spiritual, and day-to-day, it is not an obsure thing praticed by 'Wizards' or the like but by clerics and tradesmen. The world is dangerous, work is hard, and the gods may be watching. Survival and social navigation are as important as combat. The player character is nobody — and that's the point.

## 2. The Layered World

This is the most important structural fact of the setting. The city exists on top of multiple buried civilizations, each one older and stranger than the last.

### Layer 0 — The Surface City
Bronze age city-state. Living society. The world the player starts in.

### Layer 1 — The Undercity
A buried civilization beneath the city. Accessible through the lower slums via the old drainage channel. Unclear what destroyed it. Its infrastructure is still partially intact — large stone corridors, old chambers, things that were once purposeful. Spirits here are stronger than on the surface. Small vestiges of society still exist in the deeper parts.

**The old wall and palace foundations belong to this civilization.** The current city-state is squatting in its bones. The fire cult and the poison cult both most likely trace their origins here — not invented by the current city's inhabitants, but inherited, diluted, and partially misunderstood across the generations since.

### Layer 2 — The Swamp World
Beneath the undercity. A vast underground space, partially flooded, ancient. Scarred by the fall of its own civilization. The [Mushroom God] ended this world and turned it into what it is today — it was young then, and in pain, and it lashed out. Now it is old and calm and alien. The Poison Cult lives in its depths, descended from surface people who went down and never came back.

**The origin of the king's system:** Before the Mushroom God destroyed it, the Layer 2 civilization was the one that responded to the Layer 3 collapse. The Layer 3 technological world had produced death at a scale that broke the world's spiritual architecture — the dead could not pass on, soul energy was everywhere, uncontrolled, catastrophic. Someone in Layer 2 looked at this and created a solution: a system for harvesting, directing, and concentrating that energy. The mass grave that still powers the king's shrine today is here, in what is now the swamp — created by or in response to the Layer 2 civilization's last attempt to impose order on what Layer 3 had broken.

The creator of that system used it to perform a ritual on themselves. What happened is not precisely known. They may have become a god. They may have become something that a god is a good word for and a bad description of. Their society crumbled the moment they crossed over — not from external attack, not from a slow decline, but as an immediate consequence of whatever the apotheosis did. What it produced was left behind. The Mushroom God, young and in pain from the Layer 3 collapse it had absorbed the shockwaves of, ended the rest.

**What the current king believes:** The ritual worked. The creator succeeded. Apotheosis is achievable — it has been achieved, once, by someone who understood what the king is only beginning to understand. The king's household idol is, in his view, descended from whatever that being became: a god that survived its own world's end because it had transcended the conditions that destroyed everything else. This is what he wants. This is what he is working toward. He is not wrong that the ritual worked. He is wrong, in ways he cannot know yet, about what it produced.

**The Godfall — known to very few.** Somewhere beneath the swamp, or perhaps as the very floor the swamp rests on, lies the corpse of an ancient god, vast beyond comprehension. Like a whale fall on the ocean floor — a single death seeding an entire ecosystem that feeds on it for generations — every layer of the city above has been built, knowingly or not, on the remains of something divine. The mass grave, the palace foundations, the undercity, the surface city itself: all of it ultimately rests on this.

What exactly happened is not clear even to the handful who know the Godfall exists at all. What is known: a god died, and the Layer 3 technological society died with it — whether the society's collapse was the cause, the consequence, or something stranger than either is not established. The leading guess among those few is that the Layer 3 society killed it themselves — their original sin, and perhaps the true reason reality is broken where they are sealed away. But this is a guess, not a known fact.

*Open questions to reconcile later:* Is the Godfall one of the gods Layer 3 built and enslaved, or something older and separate entirely? Is the Mushroom God itself an organism that colonized the Godfall the way deep-sea life colonizes a whale fall — which would explain why the Mushroom God was merely "young" when Layer 2 collapsed, despite the Godfall predating everything? Who among current factions actually knows about it — oldest poison cult elders, the king's inner circle, no one living? Left undecided for now.

### Layer 3 — The Sealed Technological World
Deeper still. Sealed, because it has to be. The civilization here was so advanced it met its gods, built its gods, and enslaved them. Reality is somewhat broken as a result of what happened here. This world is the origin of evil spirits — they are the cursed dead of an era when humanity was at war with its own gods. Opening this world angers the [Mushroom God]. The player will disturb it eventually.

**The foundational discovery:** The act that made all of it possible — and all of it inevitable. This civilization found the soul. Not as philosophy, not as faith, but as a fact about the structure of the world: the soul is the fundamental unit of spiritual energy. It can be mapped, directed, and consumed. Gods are entities composed of or sustained by it. Once this was understood, enslaving gods became an engineering problem. This discovery is the root of every spiritual practice that came after it. Every religion in the current world is a fragment of that original technology — inherited without the underlying theory across multiple collapsed civilizations, reinterpreted as faith.

**Narrative role:** The unsealing of this layer is the structural endpoint of the main arc. The swamp is the threshold — everything the player does there is ultimately in service of finding and opening what shouldn't be opened. What drives the player to that moment will depend on earlier decisions and is intentionally left open until the path leading there is more concrete. The thematic throughline is already present: the player begins as a nobody with no god, stripped of divine lineage. They end by confronting a world destroyed because humanity tried to own its gods entirely.

### The Gradient
The deeper you go, the worse the spirit problem, the stranger the inhabitants, the older and more dangerous the magic, and the more the rules of the surface world stop applying.

### Spiritual Accumulation

Each civilizational collapse releases soul energy at massive scale — an entire world's dead, all at once, broken loose from whatever structures once contained them. Some pass on. Many cannot, or do not. The world has been accumulating this residual spiritual presence across multiple collapses. The current age is the most spirit-saturated in any recoverable history. The world is suffused with spirits. This is not incidental. It is the condition of the world.

The idol religion is not cultural tradition — it is genuine necessity, and it has become more necessary with each passing era. The spirit problem was real in the Layer 1 world. It is worse now. It will be worse still after the next collapse, if there is one. The pattern runs only one direction. The deeper you go, the more concentrated the residue — not only because lower layers are older and stranger, but because the dead of each collapse are densest where those civilizations actually ended.

**The Tower of Babel principle:** The layered world is not a tower struck down once. It is the same tower rebuilt, shorter each time, on the rubble of the last attempt, by people who understand less than the people before them did. The current city is the smallest and least-informed reconstruction yet. Its inhabitants think they live in a prosperous city. They are living in the compressed ruins of several worlds.

## 3. Religion

> **Key principle: Religion is the science of this world.** There is no secular knowledge distinct from religious knowledge. A smith who forges well is practicing religion correctly. An alchemist who understands poison is a theologian of a kind. The pictographic library is the university. Understanding this reframes every faction interaction.

### The Idol Religion — [Name TBD]
The universal practice. Almost everyone participates. Family idols are small objects, passed down through generations, that household members make small sacrifices to at mealtimes. The city's rulers consult larger civic idols. The king's god is regarded as the most powerful — not because the king is powerful, but because the king's power *comes from* the god's favor. A god's strength is a reflection of its lineage and the faithfulness of its keepers, and earthly success is a reflection of that, not the other way around.

**Key belief:** A god that has not been passed down is weak. Starting a new god from scratch is the most socially damaging thing a family can do while remaining part of society. It is mocked openly. It is also seen as the only option for those without a god — a necessary shame.

**The deeper function:** The idol religion protects against two things, and most people understand both without being able to articulate them clearly.

The first is the spirits. The dead of previous eras haunt the surface world — something that happened before anyone living can remember, for reasons no one knows. Unprotected people are disturbed in their sleep, experience waking unease, and accumulate damage from sustained exposure. Having a god in good standing keeps these at bay. This is the most immediate, practical, day-to-day function of the idol religion, and everyone in the city feels it.

The second is the nature gods. The ancient anger of the world is real, and the wilderness outside the walls carries it. Not any single god's judgment — just the ambient hostility of forces that are very old and have very long memories. Being spiritually protected does not mean nature cannot kill you. It means you are not walking into that anger naked. The idol religion provides a managed, lineaged divine presence: a god small enough to keep in the house, strong enough to matter, and reliably on your side because you feed it. The city is, in a structural sense, a collective defense against the world outside its walls. The gods of civilization are answers to the gods of nature.

Most city people don't think about it in these terms. They just know that going without a god is bad. They know it the way you know not to touch a flame — not through theory, but through the accumulated experience of everyone around them.

**Early lore function:** These two threats — the spirits and the nature gods — are the only early-game hints at the true state of the world. Neither is explained. Both are treated as facts of life. The player will eventually learn that the spirits are the cursed dead of a civilization that warred with its gods, and that the nature gods' anger is the grief of a world that has been destroyed by human ambition more than once. But in the Ditch on day one, none of that is knowable. It is simply: protect yourself, or don't sleep well.

**The nature of household gods:** Each household god is both its own distinct being and a piece of the king's god — these are not contradictory. Over generations of feeding and care, each idol develops its own character, lineage, and relationship with its keepers. They are genuine entities. They are also, structurally, nodes in the king's god's distributed network. The surface theology acknowledges the first truth — "our god is descended from the king's" — without knowing the second. The distinction matters because it means the household system is not polytheism in the ordinary sense: there is ultimately one real divine presence in the city, distributed across thousands of households, each node a real being and simultaneously a mechanism of the whole.

**The player's situation:** Slavery disconnected the player from their lineage. Their family idols were taken — seized for debt, claimed in a raid, lost. That was long ago. Society reads the player's bad luck as divine abandonment, because in this world, that's probably correct. The player is not just poor. They are spiritually unprotected in a world where that is a tangible, dangerous condition.

Being godless also means something more specific: household gods don't only protect against spirits — they route and manage what spiritual information reaches you. Without a divine lineage acting as intermediary, the player receives certain signals unfiltered that protected people never perceive at all. This is the mechanism behind the premonition dream.

**Paths to restoring divine protection:**
- Create a new god (shameful, requires going through proper channels — a blessing from the king's priests, requiring great deed or great treasure)
- Join a noble family's service and gain protection through their god (less interesting, flagged for later)
- Find something else entirely among the cults

### The King's God — The Shrine and the Secret

**What the public knows:** The king's god is the most powerful in the city because it is the oldest and most faithfully maintained. All household gods are descended from it — pieces of its power distributed across generations of worshippers. The king is its avatar: his success is the god's favor made visible. Feeding the god is how the city keeps its protection. Slaves are sacrificed at the shrine. This is accepted, known, and unremarkable. It is the cost of living in a protected city.

**What the public does not know:** The shrine atop the palace is not a place of worship. It is a machine. It channels soul energy from a mass grave in the swamp — the Layer 2 world — that has been powering this system since before the undercity was built. The Layer 1 civilization constructed the palace foundations and great wall specifically to serve as the infrastructure that connects the shrine to that grave. They understood they were tapping into something they did not create and did not fully understand. The current city is built on top of a soul-harvesting apparatus whose original architect died — or became something else — before the undercity civilization even existed.

No one in the current city knows any of this. People know a world collapsed beneath them. They live in its ruins. They do not know that the swamp below the undercity contains a mass grave of deliberate scale, or that the palace was built as a conduit to it.

The household idols are literal pieces of the king's god's power — not metaphorical descendants, but genuine concentrations of the same soul energy distributed outward across the city's population. They are also, genuinely, their own beings: each has developed distinct character and relationships across generations of care. Both things are true simultaneously. The surface theology grasps the first half ("our god descended from the king's") without knowing the structural reality of the second. When families make their daily mealtime sacrifices, a trickle feeds back upward toward the shrine. But the larger engine is the city itself: all death that occurs within it — natural, sacrificial, accidental — channels upward through the city's structure into the shrine. The populace is not simply protected by the king's god. They are, without knowing it, continuously fueling it.

**The king's lineage:** The royal family traces to the builders of the undercity — the Layer 1 civilization that raised the great wall and constructed the palace foundations as conduit infrastructure. But the lineage extends before that. The Layer 1 builders inherited the system; they did not create it. What is known within the family: they ruled in the previous world, and the world before that, and further back than either. Their god survived each collapse because it was built — or became — something that runs on death, and death is always available. The king believes his god is descended from the Layer 2 ritual creator's apotheosis. He may be correct. The god has never said.

**The shrine:** The highest point of the city. Built into the oldest part of the palace's central structure — older than the Layer 1 builders' additions, origin unknown to anyone now living. Elevated deliberately: the highest point of the city, furthest from the graves below, where soul energy rising through the layers collects and concentrates. The architecture channels what comes up from beneath.

**What the king knows:** He has a meaningful but incomplete picture. He knows his family has ruled across multiple worlds — that the Layer 1 civilization was not the beginning of his lineage, only the most recent chapter. He knows his god is very old, older than the undercity, older than anything he can verify. He knows the god runs on death. He knows the swamp exists below the undercity but believes it to be the deepest layer. He has no knowledge of the sealed technological world.

He knows — or believes, which is close enough — that the system his shrine runs on was created by someone who completed the same ritual he is preparing to attempt. That person became something that survived their world's end. The king reads this as success. His lineage traces, in his understanding, to whatever that being left behind — a god that outlasted the civilization that made it. He wants to become that. He wants to be the one who makes the crossing and comes through on the other side as something that collapses cannot touch.

He believes, with some justification, that another collapse is coming. His goal is not merely to hold power. It is to survive it in a form that does not require a civilization to prop it up. He is not wrong that the original ritual produced something that survived. He does not know what it cost, what it became, or what it has been doing since.

**The secret:** Only members of the royal family who the king has chosen to share it with know the true history. The mass graves beneath the city, the true nature of the shrine, the lineage's connection to previous ruling classes — all of this is known only within that circle. The public theology is not false exactly. It is a surface that covers something older and stranger without contradicting it.

### The Pictographic Religion — [Name TBD]
Widely respected. Second most practiced after idol worship. The symbols of their sacred script are conduits for magical energy — they direct it. Writing is worship, spiritual practice, and spellcasting simultaneously. Think Buddhist traditions around sutra copying: the act of correct inscription is itself transformative and magical.

**The Library** is literally their temple, open to the public, maintained by devotees who are very happy to explain their faith to anyone who asks.

**Talismans:** Inscribed objects imbued with magical power. The potency scales with the inscriber's skill, understanding, and spiritual development. These are the most widely worn protective objects in the city — purchased by members of all other faiths.

**Advanced practice:** Hand sign magic. Very rare. Known of but not feared — its practitioners are typically near-bodhisattva types, highly respected. Requires exceptional INT and WIL. Completely invisible to observers who don't know what they're seeing.

**Attitude toward fire cult:** Strongly opposed, both ideologically (power through sacrifice vs. power through understanding) and literally (fire destroys writing). **People with visible fire scars are not permitted in the library.** No exceptions.

**Player path:** Learn specific symbols from a master. Requisite stats and skills required per symbol. Eventually gain ability to inscribe mundane items. Hand sign magic accessible at very high stat thresholds.

**Ancient connection:** Some form of inscription practice likely originates in the Layer 3 civilization — written symbols as a technology for directing soul energy, the conduit as something more than metaphor. Surface practitioners have fragments of this in their library; their oldest symbols do things their best scholars cannot fully explain. Masters of the tradition know that more complete versions of their ancient texts exist in the undercity. They make deliberate quests there — and deeper — to recover them. What they find tends to be more powerful and less interpretable than what they practice on the surface.

### The Metal Religion — [Name TBD]
Widely accepted. Utilitarian faith. Gold has great spiritual and intrinsic value and is appropriate for decoration. Bronze is respected. Iron is the greatest known metal — sacred for its practical power, never wasted on mere ornament. The best smiths can forge magical properties into metal objects; these are used by members of all faiths.

**Practitioners:** All smiths practice this religion to some degree. The crafters district is their temple. Forges are sacred spaces. Some wise smiths invite fire cult practitioners to oversee their work spiritually — they respect the fire cult's practical philosophy even when others don't.

**Attitude toward fire cult:** Respect. Both religions are about what you can make real through direct action and will.

**Spirit-warding armbands** are among the most widely used protective items. Iron ones are considered one of the only acceptable non-tool uses of iron — a serious spiritual concession.

**The old world revelation:** When a devoted practitioner of this religion descends far enough and finds the technological ruins — machines, infrastructure, gods made of metal — it is a genuine faith crisis. Everything confirmed and destroyed simultaneously.

**Ancient connection:** The metal religion descends from a technological god of the Layer 3 world — possibly one of the entities enslaved in that era, or something that emerged from that civilization's god-building practice. The worship of metal is not arbitrary — it is surviving theology from a civilization that understood metal and what could be made from it in ways since lost. The old world revelation is not merely philosophical. It is the moment a practitioner realizes their religion has a source they never knew about. Potential extension: communities of miners and craftspeople living underground, practicing a more complete or more degraded form of this faith — to be fleshed out later.

### The Animal-Part Religion — [Name TBD]
Socially acceptable for hunters and warriors. Eyebrows raised if it's your entire identity and you live in the city. Practitioners carry and wear trophies from animals they have personally killed. The trophy's protective power is proportional to the danger of the kill. You must make your own talismans — no purchasing someone else's kill.

**Mechanical effect on equipment:** As a practitioner gains more and better trophies, they fill clothing slots with them. The religion gradually pushes practitioners toward wearing less conventional clothing and more trophies. This is not a rule imposed on the player — it is an emergent result of wanting the best items in those slots.

**The old hunter outside the gate:** An elderly man skinning an improbably large desert lizard outside the city. Too old to have done it. Clearly did. Exposition-dumps happily about the religion if the player asks questions. First available quest: kill any animal, return with any part of it. Basic survival roll required after the kill. Reward: basic trophy talisman and an introduction to the religion's logic, also a simple knife if you were unarmed.

**Ancient connection:** Probably the oldest surviving practice in the setting. It has no scholarly record because the religion does not encourage scholarship — knowledge passes through practice and oral tradition alone. Archaeological evidence of this practice exists in every recoverable layer except the sealed technological world, where it would have taken a different form. It predates the Layer 1 civilization. It likely predates the Layer 2 world. Ancient practitioners — people who understood intuitively that killing releases something, that claiming a life transfers power — may have been the original conceptual source for mass sacrifice: the logic that killing at scale releases something that can be channeled. That connection has never been made explicit in any surviving record.

### The Fire Cult — [Name TBD]
Ancient in origin — likely a practice of the civilization that built the undercity and the great wall. What survives on the surface is a fragment of something older, stripped of context and misunderstood by those who practice it and those who fear it. The undercity likely holds more intact forms of the original religion.

Seen as extreme by most. Their deepest city-dwelling devotees have small deliberate scars — visible to those who know what they're looking at, not provably magical. Not permitted in the library.

**Deep practitioners** (undercity and below): Have sacrificed limbs or more to the flame. Wear the withered husks of burned limbs. Can produce, control, or manifest fire-related powers. Barely even rumors of these individuals exist on the surface.

**Unique offering:** No tradeable trinkets. Protection is internal — basic devotees learn to spiritually protect themselves as if wearing a basic talisman from another religion. The path is about self-actualization and agency. Power comes from within, through sacrifice and will.

**Recruitment:** The fire cult does not send missionaries out to find the godless. They are simply present — visible in the Lower Ditch, practicing openly, carrying their scars without apology. The godless who end up in the Ditch find them because the Ditch is where both groups end up. First contact is player-initiated: a cultist sitting cross-legged by a clay brazier is the first person willing to speak plainly about what godlessness means and what alternatives exist.

**First contact:** A fire cult member sits openly in the Lower Ditch beside a small clay brazier. Small deliberate scars mark their hands and forearms. They also supply flame accelerant to the smith — a known working relationship between the two religions. If the player asks about protection, the cultist refers them to an elder whose hut is directly across the ditch. The elder appears as "Old Man" to the player until this referral has happened.

**Initiation:** The elder — an old man with a charred husk where his left hand was — asks the player to sit with him and gaze into his fire. Players with sufficient Occultism see something in the flames; others are told to return when ready. After the gazing, he offers to touch a burning ember briefly to the player's arm. Accepting grants the **Inner Flame** feat: permanent Level 0 spirit protection, requiring no talisman or external god. The mark does not leave you. After the initiation, the elder invites the player to sleep in his hut.

**Morning storyboard** *(after sleeping in the elder's hut)*: TBD — to be written once surrounding zone content is settled.

**Ancient connection:** Traces to the Layer 1 civilization, where it existed in a form far more developed than what survives on the surface. In the context of the soul discovery: the fire cult's theology — that power comes from life, that the soul is an animating force, that willful sacrifice burns the self brighter — is one surviving interpretation of what the Layer 3 civilization learned about the nature of the soul. A theory about what it is, preserved as religion across multiple collapses.

### The Poison Cult — [Name TBD]
Ancient in origin — like the fire cult, most likely a practice of the civilization that built the undercity and the great wall. The surface apothecary knowledge that borders on poison theology is an echo of something much older and more developed, practiced in full in the depths by people who have been doing it for a very long time.

No one openly practices this. No one invites you to meetings. Surface-level: some alchemists, apothecaries, and assassins have passing knowledge of it as a professional matter — religion is science, and if you make poisons, you've learned a little theology. Some may participate in minor practices. Few are actual devotees.

**Core belief:** Creating something akin to a philosopher's stone inside the self. Gradual immunization to increasingly powerful poisons as a path toward transcendence and ultimately immortality. [Needs further research into the historical/alchemical concept before full fleshing out.]

**Deep practitioners:** Live in the swamp beneath the undercity. Some may be descended from people of Layer 2 — not living remnants of that world, but very old. Through years or decades of escalating practice, their bodies have been transformed by what they've survived. The path requires extraordinarily high CON — the body must be able to endure what the practice demands.

Transformation progression:
- Early practice: growing resistance to poisons, minor regenerative ability
- Mid practice: no longer need to eat food (the body sustains itself differently now)
- Deep practice: significant physical toughness, meaningful regeneration, active poison-related abilities — secreting toxins, resistance becoming immunity, eventually something approaching near-immortality

These individuals are not dramatic in the way fire cult deep practitioners are, but they are not quite right either. Over time the body shows the cost of what it has processed: a gradual withering, a greening of the skin. The oldest and deepest practitioners are gaunt, faintly green, and have been alive long enough that the swamp itself feels like home. The transformation is slow enough that a practitioner may not notice it in themselves — others notice first.

**Trinkets:** Anti-poison items and a weak poison that wards off spirits. An alchemist or apothecary might sell these to a player who knows to ask. If the player has seen one of these objects, they can ask an expert in a related field about it.

**Discovery path:** Cannot be found by asking around. Must be encountered through objects first, then knowledge, then deeper questions.

**Ancient connection:** Traces to the Layer 1 civilization. In the context of the soul discovery: the poison cult's pursuit of an indestructible self — gradual hardening against death itself, the body made impervious through accumulated endurance — is another surviving interpretation of what the Layer 3 civilization learned. A different theory about what can be made permanent, about whether the soul's boundary with death can be eroded from the inside.

### The Mushroom God — [Name TBD]
Not a city religion. Not joinable from the surface. A sentient mycelial network thousands of years old. Predates Layer 2's civilization — and predates the Layer 1 civilization that built the undercity and the wall. It is older than the fire cult and the poison cult both, older than any human religious practice currently known. Was young and in pain when the Layer 2 world ended — it lashed out and turned the space into the swamp it is today. Now it is ancient and calm and deeply alien to human thinking.

Life and death mean little to it. It is a million consciousnesses and one simultaneously. It does not speak in ways humans easily understand — its followers hear its voice because they have allowed its spores to take root in their lungs and cultivated a connection over time. Priests interpret it.

Opening the sealed technological world angers it.

The society devoted to it in the deep swamp are descended from surface people, not from Layer 2's civilization, though the oldest poison practitioners may have made contact.

**Relationship with the Poison Cult:** Neighbors. The poison cult's current society at this depth is younger than the mushroom god, but the religion itself traces back to the Layer 1 civilization — meaning it is old, though not as old as the god. The accumulated poison in a practitioner's body prevents the god's spores from taking root — the primary means by which the god communes with and recruits followers. This makes poison practitioners naturally immune to involuntary absorption into the network. Both groups recognize this boundary. It is a cold, ancient, stable peace between two patient societies who have shared the same dark for a very long time.

**Relationship with the Fire Cult:** The mushroom god does not have emotions that translate cleanly into human terms. But it responds to fire cult practitioners descending to its level the way a body responds to a pathogen — not with intent or malice, but with the automatic, total reaction of a living system to something fundamentally incompatible with it. Fire and fungus. The god does not need to decide anything. The swamp simply acts. Descending as a fire practitioner means the environment itself is working against you — not out of hatred, which would almost be easier to understand.

The fire cult's theology holds that power comes from life — life as force, as will, as something that burns itself brighter by choice. The swamp is its philosophical opposite: life as consumption, as accumulation, the slow patient digestion of every civilization that came before. One is a flame that chooses to burn. The other is a rot that doesn't need to choose anything.

Deep fire practitioners are drawn to the swamp precisely because of this. The swamp is a great bowl of the world, full of the secrets and remains of fallen societies. For someone whose religion is about extracting power from life and from danger, it is an irresistible destination. The most committed go down anyway, knowing what they are walking into. The ones who survive long enough leave marks of the contest on their bodies — not corrupted, not converted, but changed by the struggle between the two forces in a way that is hard to describe and harder to look at directly.

**The deep practitioners:** Up to a few hundred years old. Gaunt, faintly green, withered. Ancient enough that the swamp feels like home even if it never fully claimed them. Recognizably human, barely.

### The Desert Storm — [Name TBD] and the Nomad People — [Name TBD]

Not a city religion. Not accessible from the city. A faction of nomadic people who live in the deep desert, in proximity to a vast, permanent sandstorm that has existed for as long as anyone can remember. City people know this group exists. They regard them with a mixture of contempt, fear, and unease — they have chosen to live beside something that could kill them, and they are not dead.

**The Storm:** Not intelligent. Not a god in the way the Mushroom God is a god. It does not communicate, deliberate, or act with intent. It simply is — a colossal permanent force that has its own patterns, its own rhythms, its own behavior. The nomads have learned those patterns over generations. They know where it will move and when. They make journeys into it on purpose, for reasons outsiders do not understand. The storm itself does not see them. But something about the practice changes people who survive it.

**What city people understand about nature gods:** The surface city's idol religion exists, in part, as a defense against things like the Storm. The gods of nature — the real ones, the ones that predate human religious history — are understood to be hostile. Not personally. Not toward anyone in particular. They are hostile the way weather is hostile, the way a landslide is hostile: without opinion, without memory, without mercy. The world itself carries a kind of anger that has never quite gone away. The idol religion keeps you in good standing with your own manageable, lineage-built god, which in turn gives you some protection from the larger, more dangerous, entirely unmanageable forces that don't care about you at all.

The Mushroom God will eventually confirm this and give it a name: the nature gods are not evil. They are the grief and the rage and the long memory of a world that has been destroyed multiple times by the thing living on its surface. The anger is not irrational. It is not the anger of something petty. It is the anger of something very, very old that has been hurt over and over and does not forget.

**Why the Storm is different from the Way of Beasts:** The Way of Beasts is not about worshipping a god. Practitioners claim power through personal acts — the kill, the trophy, the spirit of what was taken. The old hunter doesn't pray to anything. He respects the beasts he hunts. He takes from them. That is the entire theology. The nomads do something different: they approach something that cannot be owned or taken from, and they ask it — not for favor, because it cannot grant favor — but for something harder to name. Endurance, maybe. Understanding. Contact with something too large to be controlled.

**The nomads:** Tribal structure, non-fixed settlements that move in patterns around the storm. Lean, sun-darkened, heavily scarred from sand and wind. They wear wrappings that cover most skin. Their spiritual leaders are those who have made the most deep-desert journeys and returned changed. What that change looks like is hard to describe — not a transformation in the way the poison cult transforms, but a shift in affect. People who have been in the storm deeply tend to be very calm. And very hard to frighten.

**Journeys into the storm:** The primary spiritual act. Any nomad can make a journey. The depth, duration, and difficulty determine the spiritual weight of the return. Shallow journeys produce basic protection — a kind of sand-warding that functions like a talisman against surface spirits. Deep journeys produce something harder to categorize. No one who has made a true deep journey describes it the same way twice, and none of them seem eager to explain it to outsiders.

**The nomads' position on city people:** They are not hostile. They are indifferent in the way people who have been near something enormous often become indifferent to the concerns of people who have not. They trade with caravans when it suits them. They occasionally appear at the city gates, looking for specific things. They are not mysterious about what they are — they simply don't explain themselves unless asked, and they find most questions from city people mildly boring.

**The player path:** Cannot be found by asking around in the city. The nomads are not a rumor — everyone knows they exist. But actually finding them requires going into the deep desert, far beyond the coyotes and the lizards. The first contact would be a nomad camp discovered during exploration, or a nomad who has come to the city for trade. The journey-into-the-storm mechanic would be its own system — a form of exploration that requires preparation and has mechanical stakes, rewarding either basic spirit protection or deeper spiritual progression depending on how far the player goes.

**Relationship with the idol religion:** Mutual incomprehension. City people think living near the storm is suicidal and possibly sacrilegious. The nomads think city people have built very small boxes and called them gods. Neither is entirely wrong.

**Relationship with the Way of Beasts:** Respectful. Both groups are at home in environments that kill most people. They understand each other in practical terms. But the philosophies don't overlap much — one is about taking from nature, the other is about enduring something you can't take from.

### How City Religion Works as a System

The city's spiritual life operates on two distinct axes that most inhabitants navigate simultaneously.

**Axis 1 — The God:** The household idol. A divine lineage passed down through generations. This is the foundation of spiritual protection — what keeps spirits at bay at night, what grants standing in the spiritual ecosystem. Without a god, a person is spiritually exposed in a way that is practically dangerous and socially marked. This is the axis that matters most to survival.

**Axis 2 — The Practice:** The path-based religions. The Way of Beasts, the pictographic religion, the metal religion, the fire cult, the poison cult. None of these involve worshipping a god. They are disciplines — ways of developing skill, protection, and relationship with the forces relevant to one's work and life. A person can practice one, several, or none of these regardless of their household god situation. Having a practice is not required for social standing the way having a god is.

**How most city people live:** A typical inhabitant has a household god (axis 1) and practices one or more paths relevant to their lifestyle (axis 2). A smith practices the metal religion and makes offerings to their family idol. A merchant carries scribe talismans and feeds their god before important deals. A hunter keeps animal trophies and prays to their god before expeditions. These are not felt as contradictions — they are different aspects of the same spiritual world, understood from the inside as simply *how one lives*.

**From the inside, there is no tension:** Practitioners of the scribes, smiths, Way of Beasts, etc. don't think of themselves as members of competing religions. The practices are disciplines; the god question is separate. A scribe who wears an iron armband is not being inconsistent. A hunter who carries a scribe ward for a particularly dangerous trip is being prudent.

**The fire cult as exception:** Fire cult practitioners are the most likely of the city religions to practice exclusively and to abandon their household god entirely. Their theology holds that internal power replaces external dependence — the fire inside is its own protection, its own lineage. For most practitioners this is gradual; for committed ones it becomes total. Society reads this as extreme and spiritually reckless. Practically, it is — the fire cult's internal protection is real, but the household god does other things that the internal fire does not replace. The fire cult's unique offer to the godless: they don't give you a practice to supplement your god situation. They make the god situation feel irrelevant, which is not the same as solving it.

**Deep poison cult practitioners:** Those who have descended into the undercity and swamp no longer participate in the surface system at all. Their practice has consumed everything else. City-dwelling alchemists and apothecaries with passing knowledge of poison theology still maintain their household gods and understand their knowledge as professional — one practice among several, not an identity.

**The Mushroom God and Desert Storm:** Not city religions. Not compatible with the axis-1/axis-2 framework. The nomads have no household gods — they have something else entirely that the city doesn't have a category for.

**The player starts at zero on both axes.** No household god, no practice. The "no god" is the acute, dangerous, socially visible problem. "No practice" is simply being uninvested — many ordinary people are, and it carries no stigma. But the godless state has an additional consequence: being without a household god means being without the spiritual router that manages what reaches you. The player is exposed to unfiltered spiritual information that protected people never perceive. This is the mechanism behind the premonition dream.

## 4. Evil Spirits

Spirits come from previous worlds — specifically the cursed dead of eras when humanity was at war with its own gods. This is not known to current people. The city treats spirits the same way it treats other ambient dangers: as a fact of life, to be managed with the right protection. No one on the surface knows where they come from. They have simply always been there, worse in certain places, worse at night, worse the deeper you go.

**The soul connection:** The Layer 3 civilization's discovery and exploitation of the soul is the direct cause of why spirits accumulate as they do. When that civilization collapsed — when the war between humanity and its enslaved gods reached its conclusion — the dead could not pass on cleanly. The soul technology that had structured their world was violently disrupted. The cursed dead are souls that could not complete whatever transition the world's spiritual architecture once enabled. Each subsequent collapse has added more broken souls to the total. The current world is not haunted because of any single event. It is haunted because the pattern has been repeating for longer than anyone can measure, and each iteration leaves more behind.

**Surface spirits:** Relatively weak. Primarily affect sleep. An unprotected person in the slums will have their sleep disturbed — this is a game mechanic with effects TBD (fatigue, stat penalties, visions that may contain information). Protection does not need to be permanent or renewable — early game trinkets simply become outclassed as the player descends.

**The gradient:** Deeper layers have worse spirits. Surface protection does not hold in the undercity. Undercity protection doesn't hold in the swamp. What works in the swamp is something else entirely.

**The premonition dream:** The player receives a premonition of the end because they are godless — no household god to route and filter what reaches them. The dream arrives unmediated. It happens on the first protected sleep: protection is what allows real sleep, and real sleep is when the signal comes through. Content direction established; full scene TBD:
- The shrine atop the palace activates
- The ancient spiritual megastructures of the buried civilizations — the soul-channeling infrastructure of Layer 1 and deeper — begin functioning at scale
- Spirits cry out from below; the sound or sensation of something vast waking or being fed
- Glimpses of the cycles: previous worlds, previous collapses, the pattern repeating — felt rather than understood
- The imagery points toward the undercity without naming it
- Nothing is interpretable on first encounter; it is sensation and image, not information. Only descent makes it legible.
- NPCs will not be able to explain or validate what the player saw. The player has seen something that does not exist in anyone else's frame of reference.

**There is one dream, not variants.** The dream is identical regardless of how the player gained protection. What differs is the morning conversation — who the player wakes up near, and how that person interprets or fails to interpret what the player describes. Each protection path has its own morning storyboard (see Section 13 NPCs). The spirit experience before the dream is not a separate scripted event — protection simply prevents disturbance; the dream is what happens when you finally sleep deeply enough.

### Districts

**Slums**
Built into a giant ancient drainage channel leading both out of the city and down into the undercity. Small stone and wood structures built up against ancient infrastructure that is out of use. The channel is dry now. So many people live here that the city does not attempt to police the exits — hunters, adventurers, smugglers, and anyone who doesn't want to be seen use the dry channel freely.

The slums have levels. Upper levels are just poor. Lower levels get stranger. The lowest accessible part connects to the undercity proper. The player starts in the upper slums.

Worst spirit activity in the city at night. Cheapest wards sold by least skilled scribes. Fire cult most active here. The spiritually unprotected cluster here because they have nowhere else to go.

**Market District**
Nearest district to the slums. First step into the city proper. Trade, merchants, the daily business of the city.

**Docks**
Against the river. Trade by water. On the far side of the market from the slums. The lower stretches of the riverbank have stretches of salt-crusted shore — in the desert heat, river water wicks through the soil and evaporates, leaving mineral deposits. Salt is harvested and traded here. No ocean connection required; this is simply what a desert river does.

**Temple District**
Adjacent to market and docks. The library-temple of the pictographic religion is here, open to the public. The smiths and crafters operate on the edge between temple district and market/docks — their forges are temples in their own right.

**Castle District**
Accessible through the temple district. Where nobles live. The palace-temple is here — partially refurbished ruins, partially something much older. The king and his god live here. The most powerful divine lineage in the city.

**Residential District**
Borders market, docks, a little of the temple district, and the desert edge. Where ordinary citizens live.

### The Upper City Gate

A guarded checkpoint divides the city into two zones. The lower city — the Ditch, the market, the docks, and the rougher parts of the residential quarter — is where the player begins and where Act 1 takes place. The upper city — the temple district, the smiths and crafters quarter, the castle district, and the better residential neighborhoods — is physically reachable by foot but guarded at a gate.

The guards' reason is the same one that kept the player in chains: class. A freed slave with no money, no patron, and no god has no business in the upper city. This is not a locked door — it is a social boundary enforced by people with spears. The player cannot bluff, bribe, or fight their way through on day one.

**What's behind it:** The Library-Temple, the Great Forge, the castle district and palace, and the wealthier residential neighborhoods.

**Faction path implications:** The pictographic religion and metal religion both have accessible contacts in the lower city — the market scribe and the market smith — who can begin training the player. Their deeper institutions, the Library and the Great Forge, require reaching the upper city. The player knows these places exist and knows they can't go there yet.

**Unlocking it:** A specific narrative milestone, TBD. Story-gated, not skill-gated. The guards step aside because of what the player has become, not because the player passed a check on arrival.

**Design rationale:** The knowledge descent principle works horizontally across the surface city as well as vertically underground. The rough parts of the lower city give access to fringe and dangerous knowledge — the fire cult in the Ditch, the alchemist in the market. The institutional knowledge — the library's inscription tradition, the Great Forge's metallurgical theology — is locked behind the same walls that excluded the player as a slave. The player begins at the bottom of the city and the bottom of the world simultaneously.

## 7. Day One — Quest Structure

The player exits the taskmaster's room with tunic, sandals, and nothing else — no coin, no weapon, no contacts. The following opportunities exist in the immediate area. They are not prescribed in order and not all required, but the player must do *something* before they can eat, sleep with protection, or arm themselves.

**The core constraint:** Almost everything useful requires either coin or a faction relationship the player doesn't have yet. The first hour of the game is about finding the entry point that fits what the player brought out of character creation.

- A player with high STR and low everything else goes straight to the hunter.
- A player with high CHA and Deceive finds social routes into the scribe errand or the fence.
- A player who took the fire cult's offer has shelter immediately but still needs coin and a weapon.
- Every background has at least one natural entry point that doesn't require skills they don't have.


### The Old Hunter *(outside the gate)*
An elderly man skinning an improbably large desert lizard — a big desert predator, alone, clearly his kill. Too old to have done it. Tells stories if asked. Will exposition-dump on the animal-part religion enthusiastically if prodded.

**Quest:** Kill any animal. Return with any part of it. Survival roll required after the kill to dress the carcass properly.
**The kill itself:** Handled by the combat system. Small animals near the city walls are feasible targets. The player will almost certainly be fighting unarmed unless they've completed another job first and bought or stolen a weapon. A combat-built character can manage this. A scribe who tries to punch a lizard first will learn quickly to find the courier job instead.
**Reward:** Basic trophy talisman. A simple knife — first weapon. Introduction to the religion and the hunter as a recurring character.
**Natural entry point for:** Gladiator, Field Hand backgrounds. High STR or Melee investment.

### The Scribe *(market district)*
A scribe working the market — copying documents, writing letters, selling wards. Needs a document delivered to a business owner nearby. Simple errand, no skill check required, just legwork.
**Reward:** Small coin. Introduction to the pictographic religion. The scribe is both an errand-giver and an early trainer — they can teach basic inscription and sell basic wards. They are also the first person to point the player toward the Library-Temple, which is in the upper city and currently inaccessible.
**Natural entry point for:** Scribe, Domestic, Apothecary's Assistant backgrounds. Anyone with social skills or simply nowhere else to start.
**The market contact:** A business owner who receives the document. Minor NPC, to be fleshed out later. May become a source of further work.

### The Smith *(market district)*
Works a smithing stall in the market with a small forge and a home attached to the back. Needs a specific fire accelerant fetched from an alchemist in the market. The alchemist has fire cult connections — smiths and the fire cult have a known working relationship, and the smith orders his accelerant through the fire cult member in the Ditch.
**Reward:** Coin. The smith is both an errand-giver and an early trainer — he practices the metal religion and can begin teaching it. He points the player toward the Great Forge in the upper city for deeper mastery, though the player can't reach it yet. After doing work for him the player can ask to sleep in the back — he's not warm about it, but he allows it.
**Side effect:** The alchemist or someone nearby notices the player is running errands for a smith. The fire cult's approach later may feel less like a coincidence.
**Note:** Foot-operated bellows are period-appropriate (attested Egypt and Mesopotamia ~1500 BCE). Pumping bellows available as an additional minor task if needed.
**Natural entry point for:** Mason, Gladiator backgrounds. Anyone with STR.

### [The Apothecary] *(market)*
An ordinary-seeming market vendor who has clearly seen too much to be surprised by anything. Knowledgeable, pragmatic, not affiliated with any single religion but conversant in all of them professionally. First encountered either through the smith's fetch quest or by wandering the market. She is not a devotee of any path — she is a professional who knows things. That distinction matters.

**After completing her courier errand** (delivers something to the market, similar to the scribe job), she becomes talkative. If the player asks about protective charms:
- She describes every type available — scribes, smiths, animal trophies, the fire cult
- She does not mention the poison cult unprompted
- If asked whether she has anything to sell herself, she says no — but she has a potion that will protect against spirits for a few days. It tastes terrible. She's not wrong.

**The poison cult path** — slow, optional, entirely player-driven:
1. Buy the vial (1 coin). Sleep. This is the first protected rest — the dream happens here.
2. Return. On the second purchase she says: *"You know, I could teach you to make it yourself."*
3. She sends the player to gather a dusk flower (river, west of city) and a desert succulent (desert, south of drain exit). This ingredient errand is the path's equivalent of the other paths' desert tasks — already built in.
4. Player returns with ingredients → she teaches the recipe → player crafts it → she reveals it was poison all along.

The player has demonstrated curiosity and persistence — and having voluntarily drunk something awful twice, they've already decided something about themselves.

**Second visit / dream discussion:** The apothecary has no sleeping scene — the player buys the vial and sleeps elsewhere. The second encounter is when the player returns. At this point the dream is available to raise, and the teaching offer follows.

If the player brings up the dream: she listens with the expression of someone who has heard strange things before and has no particular reaction to one more. Her honest answer is *"I don't know what that means"* — not dismissive, genuinely outside her frame. She believes the player. She just doesn't have vocabulary for it. What she does know: there are people in the undercity who practice what she does as a serious spiritual discipline, not trade knowledge. They would have more of an answer. She is not connected to them — she sells remedies in the market, she is not a devotee — but she knows they exist and she knows they know more. *"Ask them. Not me. I make things that work. They make things that mean something."* Then the teaching offer.

**Toad appeasement path:** If the player encounters the toad and returns to her, she has a practical angle. She knows what deep creatures respond to — or knows enough to work it out. She sends the player into the desert to collect specific ingredients (TBD), then teaches them to brew something that will induce the toad to return home rather than fight it. No mysticism involved — applied alchemy, correctly targeted. This errand is comparable in scope and difficulty to the smith's iron retrieval and the scribe's transcription quest — it is the apothecary path's real Act 1 desert content, distinct from the quick early ingredient gather for the vial itself.

**Natural entry point for:** Apothecary's Assistant background. Anyone with INT or Alchemy investment. Anyone who asked about protection and didn't like the other options.

### [The Street Urchin] *(the Ditch, near the undercity slope)*
A strange kid — or at least young-seeming — who lives near where the Ditch starts to slope downward toward the undercity. Knows the Ditch better than anyone. Has opinions about what's down there. Will not explain how he knows what he knows.

**Role:** Introduction to theft and the criminal economy. Knows a fence. Will not point the player toward any direct source of protection — but can teach them to steal one, or help them make enough coin to buy one.

**What he offers:**
- Points the player toward a fence if they find something worth selling
- Can teach basic theft approach (Sleight of Hand check, low difficulty, tutorial for the mechanic)
- Knows things about the undercity slope that he volunteers only if asked the right questions

**Disposition:** Odd. Cheerful in an unsettling way. Not threatening. Hard to read.

### [The Dingy Inn] *(the Ditch)*
Sleep-on-straw-with-strangers dingy. The cheapest shelter in the Ditch. A bed here costs almost nothing. The owner asks no questions.

**Role:** The fallback shelter option for players who haven't found protection yet. If the player arrives here without having already encountered the fire cult missionary, the missionary is here — or someone on his behalf approaches them. The inn is a known hunting ground for fire cult recruitment. The godless end up here and someone is always watching for them.

**First night:** Sleeping here unprotected triggers the unprotected dream sequence. Sleeping here after the fire cult sermon triggers the fire cult dream. The inn is where the spirit mechanic first becomes real regardless of which path the player took to get here.

### The Fire Cult Member and Elder *(the Lower Ditch)*
The fire cult is visible in the Lower Ditch — not recruiting, just present. A lean figure sits cross-legged beside a small clay brazier, hands and forearms marked with small deliberate scars. They are the same person who supplies the smith with flame accelerant via invoice — the fire cult and the smiths have a known working relationship.

**The member's role:** Supplies the smith's accelerant order. If the player asks about spiritual protection, they explain the cult's philosophy (true power comes from within, not from gods or talismans) and refer the interested player to an elder whose hut is directly across the ditch.

**The elder:** An old man in a small hut on the east side of the Lower Ditch. His left hand is a charred husk. He appears as "Old Man" until the player has been referred by the cult member. Asks the player to sit and gaze into his fire — players with Occultism 15 see something; others are told to return when ready. After gazing, he offers to touch a burning ember to the player's arm. Accepting grants the **Inner Flame** feat: permanent Level 0 spirit protection. After the initiation, he invites the player to stay the night.

**Natural entry point for:** Any background. The player finds them by being in the Lower Ditch, not by being approached.

## 12. Trainer System

Trainers are real NPCs found through doing relevant work, following rumors, and being directed by faction contacts. They are not granted by background selection.

**Principles:**
- Trainers should feel earned
- Some skills have unconventional teachers
- Some trainers require favors, coin, or faction standing first

### Path Requirements

Each path gates deeper progression behind specific skills and stats. Players cannot invest broadly enough to advance in all paths simultaneously — specialization is required. A character can realistically go deep in two or three paths, not all of them.

| Path | Required Skills | Required Stats | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Way of Beasts** | Melee or Ranged, Survival | — | Combat + wilderness; Survival is the differentiator from Mercenary |
| **Pictographic Religion** | Occultism | INT | Sleight of Hand may also unlock some inscription content (see below) |
| **Metal Religion** | Crafting | STR | |
| **Fire Cult** | Occultism | WIL | Shares Occultism with scribes; WIL vs INT is the split between the two |
| **Poison Cult** | Alchemy | CON | CON gate reflects the physical cost of the practice |
| **Mercenary** | Melee or Ranged | — | Pure combat requirement; no spiritual or craft skills needed |
| **Thief / Criminal** | Sneak, Sleight of Hand | — | |

**Meaningful overlaps:**
- *Mercenary and Way of Beasts* both require combat investment. A fighter who also has Survival can access both. Way of Beasts adds a wilderness dimension that pure mercenary work doesn't require.
- *Fire Cult and Pictographic Religion* both require Occultism. A player who invests in Occultism can begin either path — but deeper progress requires WIL for fire cult and INT for scribes, which pull in different directions.
- *Thief and Pictographic Religion* — Sleight of Hand (thief's primary skill) may unlock some inscription content from the scribes. The manual precision of inscription has a physical component that a skilled pickpocket already has. A thief can pick up fragments of the scribe path that a pure WIL/combat build cannot touch.
- *Poison Cult and Alchemy* — Alchemy is a skill anyone can invest in, but CON gates the deeper practice. A low-CON alchemist can brew and sell remedies but cannot walk the poison path far.

## 13. NPCs

### [The Taskmaster] — Opening Scene NPC
The man who releases the player from slavery. Not cruel — dismissive. The player is already beneath his notice before the conversation ends. He represents the household that owned the player: comfortable, protected by their god, indifferent to what happens to you now that the transaction is complete.

**Role:** Delivers the opening scene. Exposition through condescension. Establishes the spirit threat and the player's divine vulnerability in the same breath, as an afterthought.

**Disposition:** Not hostile. Simply done with you. He doesn't watch you leave.

**The room:** A small functional space — gatehouse, administrative corner, somewhere that is the least important room in a building that doesn't care you exist. The door to the street is visible.

**Opening dialogue:**
> *"Well [Player Name], you're finally free. We've worked you long enough. Go make something of your life."*
> *[Laughs condescendingly]*
> *"Now that you're no longer our slave, you've lost your protection from our household deity. I guess you probably lost your own family's god sometime around when you became a slave. Did you even have a family? Don't actually answer that. Well, good luck getting to sleep tonight — the spirits are especially aggressive here around the ditch."*

**Starting gear:** The player already has their tunic and sandals — worn, theirs, the one thing that has been theirs throughout. The taskmaster gives nothing. The transaction is simply over.

**After dialogue:** He waits for the player to leave. Does not watch them go. The player steps through the door onto the street.

### [The Old Hunter] — Outside the Gate
An elderly man sitting outside the city gate skinning an improbably large desert lizard. Too old to have made that kill. Clearly did. Unhurried. Will talk at length about the animal-part religion if the player asks him anything — he's been waiting for someone to ask.

**Role:** First available quest giver. Introduction to the animal-part religion. Recurring character encountered at intervals across the whole game, not a hub the player returns to regularly. More distant and casual than other teachers — he doesn't run a school, he crosses your path. For players following the Way of Beasts, he is a distant mentor figure: a model of what the path produces at its end, encountered rarely enough that each meeting feels significant.

**Disposition:** Gruff but not unfriendly. Respects anyone willing to try something. Has seen enough to find most things amusing rather than threatening. Does not explain himself. Does not need to.

**Quest:** Kill any animal. Return with any part of it. Survival roll required after the kill — small animals near the city walls are feasible unarmed or with improvised tools. Basic trophy talisman as reward, plus a simple knife if the player arrived unarmed.

**The Toad:** If the player slept at his fire, he'll mention in the morning that he's heard there's something large and strong that's recently made its home near the surface of the undercity — blocking the passage. He wants it dead. Not because it's in his way — he has his own routes, secret paths the toad doesn't cover, and he gets where he's going regardless. He wants it dead because if the player is the kind of person who can kill it, he'd like to meet them again deeper down. It's a statement of respect framed as mild personal interest. He won't press it.

**Morning storyboard** *(after sleeping at his fire)*
Dialogue opens after waking. First presented option prompts the player to bring up the dream — framed to make asking feel natural, skippable. If the player asks: he's heard of godless people seeing things, doesn't know what it means specifically, but his read is simple — go deeper, get strong enough to go there. Points toward the undercity without elaborating. Then, as a separate beat: he mentions casually that he's heard something large has recently made its home near the surface of the undercity, blocking the passage. Says it the way he'd mention interesting weather. Implies he's already thinking about going down himself. Then flows into the existing goodbye: *"Get strong enough to go below if you want to meet again."* He leaves after the player's next rest.

**Recurring structure:** First encounter is outside the gate on day one. Later encounters are in the undercity and below — found rather than sought, in places that imply he was there long before the player arrived. Each meeting he has something new to say and something new to teach, but the player is never sent back to him on a schedule. He appears when the world opens up enough for him to be there.

**Notes:** The size of his kill is a passive signal — this is someone worth knowing more about. He doesn't explain himself. Players who ask will get stories.

### [The Market Scribe] — Market District
A working scribe in the market — copying documents for merchants, writing letters for people who can't, selling inscribed wards to anyone who asks. Accessible, practical, and quietly proud of a craft that most people treat as a service trade.

**Role:** First accessible contact for the pictographic religion path. An early trainer who can teach basic inscription technique and sell basic wards. Not a master — he will tell you directly that he is not. The Library-Teacher in the upper city is where the path eventually leads, but the player can't reach there yet, and this is not nothing.

**Disposition:** Professionally warm. He likes people who take his work seriously and is quietly dismissive of people who treat inscription as a trinket trade. He's been doing this long enough to recognize who is actually interested.

**Dream conversation:** If the player returns after the first night and raises the premonition dream, he listens carefully. His read is practical: the player received something through channels the scribes work with — meaning, transmitted through a medium, arriving in the form of image and structure rather than words. He can't get the player in there. He gives them a reason to want to go.

### [The Market Smith] — Market District
A smith working the market with a small forge built into his stall and a home attached to the back. Competent, deliberate, not given to small talk. Has a working relationship with the fire cult — the flame accelerant he uses is supplied through them, invoiced by the fire cult member in the Ditch. He doesn't advertise this.

**Role:** First accessible contact for the metal religion path. An early trainer who can begin teaching the craft as spiritual practice. Not the Forge Master — he knows this and will say so. The Great Forge is where serious practitioners go, and the player can't reach it yet.

**What he teaches:** Basic metalwork practice with spiritual grounding — not just technique but the religion's logic behind it. Enough to understand why iron matters. Requisite skills and stats apply.

**Disposition:** Gruff but not unfriendly. Respects anyone who does honest work. Warms marginally over time, in the way of someone who doesn't warm easily but does notice when the same person keeps showing up.

**Overnight:** After doing work for him, the player can ask to sleep in the back. He's not warm about it — he says something like "fine, there's a mat in the storeroom." The home is lived-in and a little cluttered; this is a real person's space. This is the only market-level path that offers direct hosting.

**Morning storyboard** *(after sleeping in his storeroom)*: He's already at the forge when the player wakes. If the player raises the dream: he stops, thinks, and then gives a brief, practical read — something about what lies below being connected to what he does, that the forge has always known the old world is down there. He doesn't have the language for it that the Library-Teacher or the Forge Master would. What he has is an intuition that has made him a better smith than most people expect a market stall operator to be. He goes back to work shortly after. Brief. Not cold, just done.

**Relationship to Great Forge:** The Forge Masters know him. He's not one of them, but he's not disrespected either. When the player eventually reaches the upper city, there will be something familiar in the Forge Master's forge — the same basic presence, elevated and given proper space.

### [The Forge Master] — The Great Forge, Temple District *(Upper City — gated)*
**Access:** Requires reaching the upper city. Not available in Act 1.

A senior practitioner of the metal religion, working at the Great Forge in the Temple District. The market smith points the player here when asked about deeper practice or spiritual protection. The Great Forge is an active forge and a temple simultaneously — the Forge Masters work through the night.

**Role:** Teacher and quest giver for the metal religion path. More experienced than the market smith; treats the craft as serious spiritual practice, not just trade.

**Quest:** Sends the player to a Layer 1 ruin that protrudes from the desert near the city. The ruin contains an ancient weapon — iron, from a previous civilization. The player reclaims the iron and returns it to the Forge Master, who repurposes and refurbishes it into the player's first iron weapon. Iron is sacred in the metal religion; this weapon has age and origin that practitioners read as spiritually significant.

**Sleeping scene:** Player can stay at the Great Forge overnight. The forge works through the night — when the player wakes, it is already active, already loud. The Forge Master is at work or moving between tasks. He notices the player is awake but doesn't make an occasion of it.

**Morning storyboard** *(after sleeping at the forge)*
If the player raises the dream: he listens without interrupting. Then is quiet — not uncertain, just thinking. His read is practical: *"What's below is what we came from. The forge has always known it. Iron doesn't come from nowhere."* He interprets it not as a warning or a vision but as the world showing the player something true — a machine starting up. He has seen old metalwork in the undercity. He knows what that implies. What he cannot do is explain the larger meaning. That is not his vocabulary. His vocabulary is metal, fire, making. *"The ruin I'm sending you to — what you find there may start answering what you saw. It won't be the whole answer."* He goes back to work.

Separately or in the same beat: the quest. Brief, businesslike. *"Iron from that world is worth more than what's in my forge now. Not in coin. In what it is."*

**Desert ruin connection:** Whether the ruin is the same one the scribes use is TBD. First physical evidence of Layer 1 the player encounters — ruins sticking out of the desert sand before they've descended underground.

**Undercity hook:** After the desert ruin quest, the Forge Master sends the player into the shallow undercity to recover more ancient metalwork — tools or fragments from the Layer 1 civilization that are worth studying or reclaiming. The undercity has better and stranger examples than anything poking out of the desert. This is his path's reason to descend.

**Toad resolution:** Kill it. The metal religion has no method of appeasement and no interest in one — the toad is a problem, the solution is force. The Forge Master expects the player to be strong enough by the time they're ready, and treats the expectation as a given. He may give the player a weapon for it or advise on what to bring.

### [The Library Teacher] — The Library-Temple, Temple District *(Upper City — gated)*
**Access:** Requires reaching the upper city. Not available in Act 1.

A senior scribe and practitioner at the library-temple of the pictographic religion. The market scribe points the player here. The library is open to the public; the teacher is happy to explain the faith to anyone who asks.

**Role:** Teacher and quest giver for the pictographic religion path. More advanced than the market scribe; understands inscription as spiritual practice and genuine power, not just trade.

**Quest:** Sends the player to a Layer 1 ruin in the desert near the city. The ruin contains ancient text — inscriptions from the previous civilization. The player transcribes the text and returns. The transcription gives the player their first inscription-based magical ability. They copy something they don't fully understand, and it works anyway. This is intentional — the knowledge-descent principle in action from day one.

**Sleeping scene:** Player can stay at the library overnight — guest space for traveling scholars and devotees. The library doesn't have a silence-at-dawn quality; it is already active, already quiet in the productive way of a place people work. Others may be stirring — other scholars, devotees, travelers given space.

**Morning storyboard** *(after sleeping at the library)*
The Library Teacher seeks the player out, or the player finds her. If the player raises the dream: she is immediately interested. She asks questions — *What did the images look like? Was there language, or something like language? What did it feel like?* She takes it seriously as received information, not delusion: someone or something transmitted meaning to the player through channels she doesn't fully understand, and she wants to know what came through. Her read: what the player describes carries structure — not written symbols exactly, but patterned meaning, the same thing inscription tries to formalize. The library holds its oldest texts precisely because its best scholars cannot fully explain what those symbols do. What the player saw may rhyme with them. More complete versions exist in the undercity; she has been trying to get a competent transcriber down there for years.

She frames the quest as collaborative: *"I want you to go to the ruin. Transcribe what you find. Bring it back. I will tell you if it connects to what you dreamed."* She isn't assigning an errand — she wants the information herself. This earns her the player who takes ideas seriously.

**Desert ruin connection:** Same ruin as the Forge Master's quest, or a nearby one — TBD.

**Undercity hook:** The transcription from the desert ruin is a fragment — a surface exposure of something older and more complete underground. After the player returns with it, she tells them directly: there are inscriptions in the shallow undercity that predate anything in the library, and she has been trying to get someone down there who can copy them. The player goes, transcribes, brings back. Each layer of descent produces older and stranger text. This is the pictographic religion's knowledge-descent principle in practice.

**Toad resolution:** A method exists, TBD in detail. The scribes' approach will involve inscription or text — either a symbol that compels the creature, or knowledge of what it is and how to communicate with it. The Library Teacher will know that the toad's presence is not random; she won't know why it's there yet, but she'll treat it as something to be understood rather than simply killed.

### [The Market Thief] — Market District
A pickpocket working the market crowd. Not visible unless the player makes a successful Perception or Sleight of Hand check — attentive players and thief-builds find him naturally; everyone else walks past without knowing he exists. Once spotted the player can confront him or simply watch him work. Either is a valid path to introduction.

**Role:** Entry point to the city's criminal economy. Replaces the removed urchin character and absorbs that role entirely. Knows the Ditch, knows the fence, knows which guards can be bought and which can't. Introduces the player to pickpocketing as a mechanic — his tutorial is him demonstrating it, which the player just watched.

**What he offers:**
- Pickpocketing tutorial (teaches the Sleight of Hand mechanic in practice)
- Introduction to the fence (a separate NPC, location TBD)
- Knowledge of the Ditch and the dry channel routes
- Eventually: points toward the criminal network centered in the shallow undercity

**Disposition:** Relaxed, a little amused at being caught. Not hostile — being spotted by someone perceptive enough to notice is more interesting to him than threatening. Treats the player as a potential colleague rather than a problem.

**Criminal underground path:** The thief's contacts eventually lead the player to a criminal operation centered in the shallow undercity — not too deep, well away from the faction content further down. The dry channel that runs through the Ditch is already a known smuggling route; the underground operation uses it.

**Quest progression:** Crime work that escalates through the criminal network — and eventually surfaces information about the king's ritual that nobody in the network understands the value of.
1. Pickpocketing tutorial in the market — the thief demonstrates, the player tries
2. Introduction to the fence — first fence job (move a stolen item, no questions asked)
3. Escalating fence jobs — specific targets, specific items, increasing coin and risk
4. Criminal network in the shallow undercity — more serious work, access to people the fence couldn't introduce
5. The information — through a job (a break-in, a stolen document, an overheard conversation in a place the player wasn't supposed to be), the player comes across something about the king's ritual. Fragmentary, not fully legible without context, but enough to understand that something is being prepared and that it involves the shrine and what lies below. The criminal network doesn't know what they have.

**The fork:** The information implies two options the player could pursue — usurp or stop.
- *Usurp:* Understand the ritual well enough to position themselves to benefit from it, redirect it, or seize the power it would produce. This requires actually learning what the ritual does, which means descending far enough to find out — the thief path's version of the main arc, entered through greed rather than prophecy.
- *Stop:* Use the information to disrupt the king's plan. Requires understanding what's at stake — again, requires descent. The thief who goes this route is doing it for their own reasons (political leverage, contract from a rival, simple self-preservation once they understand what the ritual actually does), not out of spiritual conviction.

Neither option is forced. A player who wants to stay in crime work can ignore the information entirely or sell it to someone else and walk away. The fork only opens for players who engage with it.

**What the criminal network knows:** Nothing about prophecies or spiritual meaning. They know the king has secrets, which is not a surprise — everyone knows the king has secrets. The specific information the player finds is above their pay grade and they know it. They are relieved to have it off their hands.

Someone in the market who places fighters for coin. Has the look of a person who has seen too many capable people come and go. Approaches the player or is findable by asking around — someone always knows who to talk to about work.

**Role:** Entry point to the mercenary path for combat-focused players who don't want to engage with the religious faction systems. First contact, audition, and ongoing quest giver as the player's reputation grows.

**First meeting:** Clocks the player immediately as a nobody — no reputation, no known work, no referral. He sends the player solo when everyone in his world assumes you'd need a group for this.

**The bandit camp:** Located near the city on a well-traveled route — desert or plains adjacent. A real combat challenge for an underprepared player. The bandits aren't just blocking a road; they have a camp, they have numbers, and clearing them is a meaningful fight. This is the mercenary path's Act 1 desert content.

**Disposition:** Transactional, not unfriendly. Respects results. Has no interest in why the player did something, only whether it got done. Warms slightly after the bandit camp, in the way that people who deal in violence warm to competence.

**Quest progression:** Escalating mercenary work that eventually feeds into royal service — and from there, into helping the king complete his ritual. The player on this path never receives the spiritual context. They are simply the best tool available, and the chain of command eventually reaches the top.
1. Bandit camp — the audition
2. Escalating contracts from the fixer — caravan protection, bounty work, debt collection, increasingly serious clients
3. Noble clients — the fixer's better work comes from people with money and problems; the player starts doing work for the city's upper class
4. Royal service — the player's reputation reaches the king's household; they are recruited for work that requires someone capable and unaffiliated with any of the factions the king needs to manage carefully
5. The ritual — the player ends up as muscle (or more) for the king's completion of the shrine ritual; the exact role is TBD, but they help it happen

**The structural irony:** The mercenary player never gets the warning the spiritual players received. They have no premonition, no NPC pointing them toward the meaning of what lies below, no framework for understanding the ritual as anything other than royal business they are being paid to enable. They may have heard things — everyone in the city knows the shrine exists — but they have no reason to connect it to the state of the world. They help complete the thing that the rest of the game is about stopping or surviving.

**Whether this is recoverable:** TBD. Whether the player can realize what they've done and change course, or whether this path locks certain consequences, is a later design question. The path itself ends at royal service and ritual completion. What that means for the endgame depends on how the larger arc resolves.

## 15. The Knowledge Descent Pillar

This is a core design principle that should inform every layer of content.

**The core idea:** Incredible amounts of knowledge have been lost to time. The player can learn the true history of the world — but only by descending. Surface understanding is fragmentary, wrong in places, and filtered through a thousand years of oral tradition and institutional forgetting. The deeper you go, the older and more complete the knowledge.

### How this manifests layer by layer

**Surface (Layer 0):**
- The fire cult is seen as extreme, dangerous, a fringe group. Surface practitioners don't know their own religion's full depth.
- The poison cult is barely known to exist. Apothecaries have working knowledge without understanding what they're participating in.
- The old wall and palace foundations are treated as facts of the landscape. No one asks who built them.
- The idol religion has absorbed and overwritten much of what came before. Most people believe their version is the original.
- The pictographic religion has fragments of much older knowledge in its library — but even its scholars disagree on what the oldest symbols mean.

**Undercity (Layer 1):**
- The civilization that built this place practiced fire and poison religion in forms far more developed than anything on the surface. Written records may survive. Ritual spaces may be intact.
- Players who have learned fire or poison theology from surface contacts will start to see their surface knowledge is simplified — either watered down intentionally for new initiates, or just decayed.
- NPCs who have lived in the undercity long enough (smugglers, outcasts, long-term squatters) may have picked up things they don't fully understand.
- The spirits here are stronger. Surface wards start failing. Characters who ask *why* their ward stopped working may learn something about how protection actually functions.

**The Swamp (Layer 2):**
- The poison cult in full, developed form. Deep practitioners who have been doing this for generations. The surface alchemist's three-drink entry point is a rumor of a rumor compared to what exists here.
- The mushroom god's society. Not hostile by default — but communion requires the god's spores to take root, which means being here long enough and being the right kind of open. Fire cult practitioners are physiologically incompatible. Everyone else is just... slow.
- The remnants of Layer 2's civilization give context to why the swamp exists — the mushroom god didn't create the space, it just *filled* it after something else ended.

**The Sealed World (Layer 3):**
- The truth about where evil spirits come from.
- The truth about what "building your gods" actually did.
- Why the mushroom god lashed out when it was young — what it was responding to.
- Whether the fire and poison cults' surface fragments contain knowledge that was *deliberately* transmitted downward to preserve it, or whether it just survived by accident.

### Implementation note
This doesn't require special mechanics beyond what the game already supports. It requires writing. Every faction contact, NPC dialogue line, and found text object should be written with awareness of which layer's version of the truth it represents.

## 16. Map & Zone Redesign

### What the current system does
The game uses a tile-based isometric map with discrete zones (TileScene instances). Each zone is a self-contained tile grid. The player and enemies move between cells within a zone. Zone transitions are possible (currently only one zone in use).

**The existing system should still work.** The question is how to redesign the zones themselves to match the new world structure.

### The challenge
The current starting zone is a placeholder — a flat outdoor area with no meaningful layout. The new design requires:
- Multiple distinct city districts, each with a different feel and different NPCs
- Vertical depth — the world goes *down*, not just across
- The drainage channel as a physical path that connects surface → undercity → swamp
- Zones that feel geographically coherent as parts of a larger city

### Proposed zone structure

```
[Desert Edge / Outside Gate]    ← Old Hunter just outside
         |
  [Ground Exit Path]            ← slopes upward, opens to daylight
         |
       [Fork]                   ← near the top of the Ditch; ancient stone junction
      /      \
[Upper Ditch / Starting Area]   [Lower Ditch slope]
← Manor back door (Taskmaster)       ↓
← City streets (only lateral exit)  [Undercity access]
← Inn, Urchin, Missionary, NPCs          ↓
                                    [Undercity]
                                         ↓
                                  [Deep Undercity]
                                         ↓
                                   [Swamp Entry]       ← surface wards fail here
                                         ↓
                                    [The Swamp]        ← poison cult in full; mushroom god
                                         ↓
                                  [Sealed World]       ← endgame; do not open
```

The rest of the surface city connects laterally from the Upper Ditch:
```
[Upper Ditch] → [City Streets] → [Market District]    ← Alchemist, Market Scribe, Market Smith
                                        |
                              ~~[Upper City Gate]~~    ← Guarded; story-gated, not open in Act 1
                                        |
                               [Temple District]       ← Library-Temple, Great Forge
                                        |
                               [Crafters District]     ← Senior smiths, sacred forges
                                        |
                               [Castle District]       ← Palace, king's god, nobles
```

### City Cardinal Orientation

- **North** — nicer districts, castle, nobles, temple
- **West** — river (large, trade-bearing; the city's reason for existing)
- **South** — the Ditch, the descent, the undercity
- **East** — desert edge, residential, the less interesting direction

The Ditch runs roughly north–south through the lower city. The river is close enough to the western wall that the Ditch's ground-level exit pipe emerges on a strip of land between the city wall and the water — dry ground, not the river itself.

**The Eastern Mountains — [Name TBD]:** Far across the desert to the east, a mountain range rises at the edge of the known world — the practical edge of the map. The desert simply runs out into foothills and then stone. Nobody from the city has mapped what's beyond it, and nothing in the design so far requires that they ever could. The foothills and the start of the range are explorable: a handful of visitable locations TBD (a watchtower, a mining camp, a hermit's perch — whatever fits once the area is built out). Distance and the journey there are the point; what's nestled at the base of the mountains is the reward for crossing the desert to find it.

### Zone Edge Indicators

Every zone transition that changes depth needs a visible edge marker so the player always knows which direction is deeper. Two components:

- **Wall height gradient** — the primary signal; walls visibly taller/shorter as you approach the edge
- **Edge marker** — a small directional indicator at the zone boundary (arrow or icon, style TBD) with a label: *"Deeper ↓"* / *"Surface ↑"* / *"Outside →"*

These should be diegetic where possible — an old carved marker in the stone, a hanging sign, something that fits the world. But a clean UI fallback is fine for the first build.

### The Ditch — Zone Layout (First Build)

```
         [N — Manor back wall / city streets]
                      |
               [Upper Ditch]
            Inn · Urchin · Missionary
                      |
                   [Fork]
                  /       \
        [SW — Exit pipe]   [S — Descending path]
        walls shrink        walls grow, darkens
        daylight ahead      no NPCs past here
              |                    |
     [↑ Outside / Old Hunter]  [↓ Undercity]
```

For the initial build the Ditch only needs five connected nodes. Geometry is minimal; the goal is to establish the structure and transitions, not fill in every tile.

**Node 1 — Manor Back Wall**
The taskmaster's door. A stone wall belonging to a building that doesn't care about the Ditch — it just happens to back onto it. The door is a servant's exit or drainage gate. Slightly elevated above the channel floor; a few steps down brings the player to the Ditch proper. No need to define the manor beyond its back face.

**Node 2 — Upper Ditch**
The inhabited stretch between the manor wall and the fork. Walls already tall but not yet oppressive — a sliver of sky still visible. The Inn is here. The Urchin hangs around the area. The Missionary approaches here or at the Inn. This is where the spirit problem is worst at night (the godless cluster here). Crowded, torchlit, cramped.

**Node 3 — The Fork**
The channel visibly splits. Ancient stone, probably a collapsed arch or old channel marker — something that implies this junction was once deliberately built and controlled. The fork is close enough to the top that the player can see it almost immediately on entering the Ditch. Both paths are visible from here.

**Node 4 — Ground Exit Path (southwest)**
The fork branch that slopes upward toward the southwest. Walls decrease in height; daylight becomes visible ahead. The original surface drainage exit — the pipe mouth opens onto a strip of dry land between the outer city wall and the river. Transitions to the outside-gate area. Zone edge marker: *"Outside ↑"*. The Old Hunter is just outside.

**Node 5 — Descending Path (south)**
The fork branch that continues directly south. Darker immediately — the wall height jumps noticeably past the fork. No NPCs past the fork on this path in the first build. Ends at the Undercity zone transition. The transition marker should feel like a threshold: older stone, different construction language, the channel becoming something not built for human habitation. Zone edge marker: *"Deeper ↓"*.

### Zone design principles
- **The Ditch** runs as a physical channel through the lower part of the city. It is both a geographic feature (the old drainage channel) and a social zone (where the unprotected live). Moving along the Ditch is how you go deeper.
- **Districts are connected laterally** — the player can move between them freely at the surface. The game opens up sideways before it opens up downward.
- **The vertical path is the main arc.** Lateral exploration on the surface is the first act. Descent is the second act and beyond.
- **Each zone transition deeper should feel like crossing a threshold** — different lighting, different spirit activity, wards that don't work here anymore, NPCs who are unsettled or changed.

### The Ditch — Visual Language
**Primary references:** The city from *Conan the Barbarian* (1982) — dense ancient stone, torchlit, lived-in and filthy — crossed with Blighttown from *Dark Souls 1* — massive vertical shaft, rickety wooden scaffolding built against ancient stone walls, going deeper means going darker.

**The Ditch is an ancient stone drainage channel**, built by the Layer 1 civilization, that has been colonized over generations by people with nowhere else to live. Wooden platforms, rope bridges, and lean-tos are built against and between the ancient stone walls at every height.

**The slope mechanic:** One full traversal of a Ditch zone equals descending one conceptual layer. The floor tiles stay flat (isometric grid), but the ancient stone walls on both sides get progressively taller as the player moves deeper. The visual channel narrows and deepens as you go. This makes the slope readable without requiring engine-level elevation changes.

- **North end of zone (upper Ditch):** Walls 1–2 tiles tall. Slivers of open sky visible above. Dense, crowded, fire everywhere, market stalls crammed against the stone. Fresher wood on the scaffolding.
- **South end of zone (lower Ditch):** Walls 4–5 tiles tall and looming. No sky. Rotting platforms. Torches sparse and far apart. Fewer people — and the ones present are not people you want to look at directly.
- **Zone transition:** The south end of the Ditch zone steps into the Undercity, which begins at the same wall height the Ditch ended at. The Undercity never has open sky. The Ditch still had slivers at the top.

**Lighting design:** NPC density and torch density both drop as you move south. Upper Ditch is loud and lit. Lower Ditch is quiet and dark. This is a gradient, not a hard line — the change is gradual enough that the player may not notice exactly when it shifted.

**Zone width and building density:**
The zone uses the full ~80-tile width, with the ancient stone channel walls running along the outer edges. Buildings constructed against the inside of those walls are what make the space feel cramped and populated — not a narrow zone, but a full-width zone filled in by human construction. The navigable floor is whatever remains between the buildings.

- **Upper Ditch:** Buildings dense on both sides. Stalls and lean-tos encroach toward the middle. Natural variation — wider pockets where a building ends, narrow squeezes where two structures nearly meet. Feels packed.
- **Near the fork:** Slightly more open. The junction needs to be spatially readable — both paths visible from the fork tile.
- **South path:** Building density drops immediately past the fork. A few structures, then nothing — just the ancient stone walls and the channel floor. The emptiness is the point.

### Building Interiors — Two Tiers

**Type A — Separate interior zone**
Large or atmospherically distinct buildings. Stepping on a door tile loads a new TileScene the same way outdoor zone transitions work. The interior does not appear on the world map — needs an `is_interior: true` flag (or equivalent) on the zone to skip world map registration. Entry and exit points are door tiles rather than zone edges.

*Use for:* The inn (multiple rooms, beds), the library-temple, the smith's forge, the alchemist's shop, any space that should feel like a distinct location.

**Type B — Same-zone room**
Small structures where the interior is just a few wall tiles forming a rectangle, with one walkable gap as the door. No new systems required — already possible with `blocks_movement` wall tiles. The "room" is still part of the parent zone and renders alongside everything else.

*Use for:* Vendor stalls with a back room, small shacks, shrines, storage rooms, anything where stepping inside doesn't need to feel like a location change.

**Rule of thumb:** If you'd want different lighting, a different ambient mood, or the player to feel like they've "gone somewhere" — Type A. If it's just a room attached to a building face — Type B.

### The Layer 1 Desert Ruin
A ruin from the civilization that built the undercity and the great wall, partially exposed above the desert sand near the city. The first physical evidence of Layer 1 the player encounters — visible before they've descended underground. It is old in a way that reads immediately: wrong proportions, stone that doesn't match anything in the current city, partially buried, partially standing.

**Function:** Quest destination for both the Forge Master and Library Teacher paths. Whether the smith and scribe quests use the same ruin or adjacent ones is TBD.

- *Metal religion:* An ancient iron weapon is somewhere inside or embedded in the structure. Iron from a previous civilization, abandoned when the world ended. The player retrieves and returns it; the Forge Master refurbishes it into the player's first iron weapon.
- *Pictographic religion:* Ancient inscriptions on the walls — text from the Layer 1 civilization. The player transcribes them. The transcription yields the player's first inscription-based magical ability, copied from something they don't fully understand.

**Tone:** Unsettling in a quiet way. No enemies required for the first visit, though the desert surrounding it is dangerous. The ruin itself should feel like a place that was built for purposes the current world has no vocabulary for. Nothing explains it. Players who come back later, after learning more, will read it differently.

**Narrative role:** First hint that the city is squatting in the bones of something older. The premonition dream pointed somewhere. This is the first concrete thing that rhymes with it.

### What needs redesigning
- All current zones replaced or repurposed — the current zone_north setup is a prototype, not a final layout
- New art direction per zone (isometric tile palette, color mood, density of structures)
- Zone transition system may need a "depth" property — what level of protection is required to function here at night
- The existing TileScene / pathfinding / combat systems are all reusable; it's the *content* of zones that changes, not the engine

## 18. Act 1 Structure — The Toad

### Shape of Act 1

**Surface arc:** Day one quests → protection → first night / premonition dream → morning conversations → all paths point toward the undercity.

**Shallow undercity:** Player descends. Open fire cult practitioners near the entrance. Strange atmosphere, stronger spirits. Exploration and early discovery.

**The roadblock:** A very large toad from the depths has taken up residence in a key passage leading deeper into the undercity. The player encounters it and cannot pass — it will kill them on approach and has exceptional detection range. Cannot be sneaked past. NPCs in the shallow undercity warn about it; the encounter itself confirms the warning.

**Turned back:** Player returns to the surface. The early game sidequests — desert hunting, market work, faction errands, exploration — now have a purpose. The player gets stronger, earns coin, finds better gear.

**Desert arc:** Most of the early sidequest content that points outward from the city points toward the desert. The desert is dangerous (requires basic protection to survive at night) but traversable. No single specific item or key is required from the desert — the arc is about capability: levels, gear, money, and potentially the nomad faction if the player goes far enough.

**Resolution:** Player returns ready to deal with the toad. Two paths, and both converge on the same outcome — the passage opens either way:
- **Kill it** — the primary path. Requires the strength and method gained through the desert arc and surface quests.
- **Appease it / send it home** — an alternative. The poison cult and the scribes each have a method for getting the toad to return to the depths. Details TBD per faction.

**Act 2 begins:** The passage is clear. The real undercity opens.

---

### The Toad

A creature from the deep layers — the swamp or below — that has been drawn upward and taken residence in a passage near the undercity surface. It did not come up on its own; the spiritual machinery of the king's shrine activating has disturbed something below. The player will eventually be able to piece this together but will not know it on first encounter.

It has no name and isn't unique — it's an ordinary animal of its kind. Down at the depth it actually belongs to, a toad this size is unremarkable, just another resident of the deep undercity/swamp ecosystem. It only reads as a monstrous, dangerous anomaly because the surface world has never had to deal with something this large before. At first encounter, the player has no way to know this — it's simply an enormous, dangerous animal in a place it should not be.

**Mechanical properties:**
- Blocks a key passage physically
- Exceptional detection range — approaching triggers combat well before visual contact
- Cannot be navigated around or sneaked past at the player's Act 1 capability level
- Lethal on encounter for an underprepared player

**Resolution paths:**
- *Kill:* Combat method and required preparation TBD. The Old Hunter wants this outcome and will look forward to meeting the player again deeper down if they accomplish it.
- *Appease:* Getting it to return home — not killing it, but removing it. Each method is a substantial errand comparable in scope to the smith/scribe ruin quests — this is Act 1 desert content, not a quick task. Poison cult method: see **Swallow** below — this is how the poison cult's appeasement actually resolves mechanically. Scribe method TBD. Possibly others.

**Swallow (combat ability, decided 2026-06-22):** Mid-fight, the toad can swallow the player whole. While swallowed, the player takes damage over time (being digested), but the toad itself becomes more vulnerable in return — a real risk/reward window rather than a death sentence. The interior will use the small-interior-room building block discussed long ago but never actually built — a literal tiny room representing the toad's stomach.

This doubles as the poison cult's appeasement method: the player either douses themselves in something beforehand (so getting swallowed becomes the delivery mechanism) or feeds it to the toad directly once inside. Either way, it's appeasement *through* being eaten rather than around it. What the player douses themselves in/feeds it, exact damage numbers, and how much more vulnerable the toad becomes are all still TBD.

Being swallowed may also double as a traversal shortcut — surviving/escaping from inside could deposit the player at an alternate Act 2 starting location, distinct from the normal "passage opens" route. Not decided whether this applies to the kill path, the appease path, or both.

**Downstream consequences (to be fleshed out):** Both paths converge on the same gate result — passage clear, Act 2 opens. They diverge only in flavor/faction reaction: kill removes it permanently and the Old Hunter takes a personal interest in the player; appease sends it back down alive, deepens the poison-cult relationship, and leaves open the possibility it (or one like it) is encountered again later, deeper down where it actually belongs.

**Narrative function:** First evidence that the premonition dream is already real — the machinery is moving, things are being disturbed, the cycle has started. The player does not have the context to read it this way yet.

---

### The Old Hunter and the Toad

He is not blocked by the toad. He has his own routes — secret paths, knowledge of the undercity's older geography. He gets where he is going regardless of what's living in the main passage. He mentions the toad to the player not as a problem he needs solved but as a filter: if you can get past that, I want to meet you again. It is respect, stated as mild personal interest. He will be found deeper in the undercity by players who clear the way.

## 19. Cooking & Food System

### Overview

Cooking is a skill governed by Intelligence. It gates two distinct things: what meal you can produce at rest time (which determines what buffs you receive the following day), and what preservation recipes you can craft between rests. The system is designed so a player with zero cooking skill still gets by — their meals are simple and plain, and their food spoils fast, but they are not locked out of the rest mechanic. Investment in cooking raises the quality of what you produce and opens up specific named recipes with specific named effects.

---

### Food Spoilage

Raw meat expires after **2 rests**. The counter ticks down each time the player takes any rest — camping, inn, or elder. When it hits zero the item is silently removed from inventory. This is a direct parallel to how `vial_protection_rests` decrements after each rest. Practical effect: raw meat is a use-it-or-preserve-it resource. Two rests is enough time to decide, but not enough to let it sit forgotten.

Preserved food items produced through cooking recipes have their own longer expiration timers, defined per recipe. That is the mechanical payoff for investing in preservation.

---

### Cooking at Rest (Camping Only)

Inn and elder rests skip the cooking step — a meal is provided. The provided meal counts as a **basic meal** (see below) and grants no additional buff beyond standard rest.

At a camping rest, the player slots a food item and a cooking quality roll fires: `1d100 + cooking modifier` (INT-derived). The roll determines what tier of meal is produced (0–5, reusing the existing quality scale). The raw food is consumed; no meal item enters the inventory — it is eaten immediately.

**What can be used as a base food item:** Any item classified as a "standard food" — hunted meat, preserved food from crafting, certain plants. Small items (insects, herbs, items that function as seasonings) do not qualify on their own. The system should make it clear in the UI what counts.

---

### The Basic Meal

The floor result — available to any player regardless of cooking skill. Provides the standard rest outcome: full HP and Spirit restore, exhaustion cleared (if spirit protection was met). This is identical to the current rest result. The cooking system adds on top of this floor; it does not threaten it. A player who never invests a single point in cooking still gets the same rest they always have.

What cooking skill does is unlock the tiers above the floor — meals with named effects that persist into the next day.

---

### Specific Recipe Meals

Each recipe is designed individually. Every recipe produces a specific named meal and a specific named buff — to be fleshed out per recipe. Recipes are gated by cooking skill level, similar to smithing recipe gates.

A small number of starting observations to design toward:
- Meals using better ingredients (higher quality meat) should have higher buff ceilings
- Some recipes will require multiple ingredients (meat + a plant, meat + a preserved component)
- Nomad-derived recipes may use ingredients not found in the city
- Some recipes may be hunter-adjacent — taught by the Old Hunter, or found in the Layer 1 desert ruin

**Current recipe list: TBD.** Individual meals will be designed case by case.

---

### Preservation (Crafting Menu)

Preservation recipes appear in the normal crafting menu under a new `cooking` category, parallel to `alchemy` and `smithing`. These produce food items with extended `expires_in_rests` timers.

**Smoke Meat** — takes any raw meat and produces **Smoked [Beast] Meat**. Requires no special ingredient — the player is already making fires to rest, and smoking is an extension of that. Gated by Cooking 8 or Survival 15 (either qualifies). Keeps as long as salt-cured meat, and carries no passive buff of its own, but whatever rest recipe it's later cooked into comes out **20% more effective** across the board.

**Salt-Cure Meat** — requires **salt** as an ingredient and a higher cooking skill threshold (15). Produces a preserved item with the same long expiration timer as smoked meat, plus a small passive buff (Salted Provisions, +3% max HP) if eaten plain at rest without being cooked into a recipe. Salt is gathered directly from the mineral deposits along the riverbank (see Section 6) — it is free but finite. A player who has been to the docks recently will have a supply; one who has been descending into the undercity or crossing the desert will eventually run out and fall back to smoking until they return to the surface.

Smoking and salt-curing are now both available from the Meat-Hawker, who sells raw, sun-dried, salt-cured, and smoked cuts of coyote, lizard, and sand beetle meat directly, and teaches all three preservation recipes.

Other preservation methods (fermentation, drying, specific nomad techniques) are TBD and will be added as the world content is designed.

---

### Fishing

A fishing rod is a standard inventory item that can be slotted to the hotbar. Activating it while standing at a **disturbed water node** on the river triggers a survival roll to catch a fish. Disturbed water nodes are specific tiles on the river — visible surface disturbance marking where fish are active. Not every stretch of riverbank qualifies; the player has to find the spots.

The catch is a fish item that functions as a food item — usable at rest time for a cooking roll like any meat, subject to the same 2-rest spoilage timer. Fish likely have their own quality tiers from the survival roll (same carve-roll pattern: roll determines quality 0–5). A ruined catch is still a catch.

**The fishing rod** — where it comes from is TBD, but the docks district is the natural source (a vendor, a fisher NPC, or craftable). It is a tool, not consumed on use.

**River vs. other water** — whether fishing nodes exist in the undercity or swamp (underground water sources) is TBD. Mechanically interesting; thematically the fish down there would be something other than ordinary fish.

---

### Spiritual and Cultural Dimension

Cooking knowledge in this world does not belong to any religion or institution. It is domestic, private, and generational — passed from mothers to children, from travelers to those who feed them, from nomads to anyone who earns the exchange. The knowledge carries something more than technique: specific recipes prepared with the right understanding have effects that cannot be explained by the ingredients alone. This is not a named practice. Nobody calls it magic. It is simply that some people know how to prepare food properly and some people do not, and the difference is felt.

This connects to the broader spiritual accumulation in the world — not as a religion, not as an organized practice, but as one of many ways that knowledge from older layers survives in the present without anyone quite knowing where it came from. A nomad grandmother's recipe for fermented lizard is, somewhere below its surface, a fragment of technology no one in the current age could reconstruct.

**The innkeeper** is the most likely teacher NPC for this system in the Ditch. She has fed travelers from every background, taken in the godless and the desperate, and accumulated fragments of more traditions than she could name. She does not think of herself as a teacher of anything sacred. She thinks of herself as someone who knows how to feed people. The player accesses the cooking skill tree through her in the same way they access other faction knowledge — by earning the relationship. Her full NPC profile is TBD, but she belongs in the Ditch, specifically at or near the Inn.
